On November 19, 2026, at the Institute of Informatics of the Federal University of Goiás (UFG), in Brazil, I received my **AI Global Voice** certificate with *Academic Distinction*.

AI Global Voice is an English program for artificial intelligence researchers, created by three institutions. The first is the **Regional English Language Office (RELO) of the U.S. Embassy**, the embassy's regional office for English language teaching. The second is **CEIA/UFG**, the university's Center of Excellence in Artificial Intelligence. The third is UFG's **School of Letters**. In the words of the call for applications, the goal is to "strengthen the international profile of researchers": to prepare people who will present at conferences, symposia and competitions abroad, and people who want to take a startup overseas.

This was the first edition, and there were few seats. There were two classes of at most 15 people each, applicants needed B2-level English or higher, proven by a certificate or a test, and the final list had 22 names. Over two months, from September 8 to November 5, we had 32 hours of in-person classes on Tuesdays and Thursdays. At the end, my certificate recorded that I completed every activity with distinguished development among my peers.

> **In 30 seconds:** AI Global Voice is a program by the U.S. Embassy, through RELO, with CEIA and UFG's School of Letters, for AI researchers who will speak English outside Brazil. In 32 hours, we practiced networking, three presentations with recorded feedback, rhythm and pronunciation, body language and cross-cultural communication. I closed the course with an academic presentation about my own second brain method and received the certificate with Academic Distinction.

In this post, I first describe how the course worked. Next, I cover what I learned beyond the language, the English itself and the three presentations, including mine. Finally, I thank the people who made the first edition happen.

## How the course worked

Our instructor was **Carol Binder**, who is affiliated with UFG's School of Letters and is a Fellow of the U.S. Embassy. Before Goiânia, she taught English to university students in Singapore, South Korea and Vietnam, and she was an examiner for IELTS, one of the world's main English proficiency tests.

Each class came with a script written by the teachers, with dialogues, exercises and model phrases. We read aloud, practiced in pairs and then presented to the group. One classmate recorded each presentation, and at least one other had the job of asking for clarification, just as someone would at a conference. Then the class and the instructor gave feedback on the same five points: structure and timing, body language and eye contact, transitions, rhythm and pauses, and clarity of pronunciation. In addition, there were one-on-one sessions on Wednesdays.

## What I learned beyond English

The course was not only about the language. A large part of it was about how people from different cultures communicate, and that was the part that stayed with me the most.

**High-context and low-context cultures.** The class started with a case. John, an American, tells his Japanese colleague Hiro that his proposal "is okay, but it needs a lot of work before it's ready." Hiro feels deeply insulted and stops taking part in the project. However, John was not trying to be rude: he was simply being direct. The explanation is context. In low-context cultures, such as the United States and Canada, the message lives in the words, and feedback is explicit and literal. In high-context cultures, such as Japan, much of the meaning lives in what is not said, in the relationship and in the care not to make anyone lose face. Brazil sits on the high-context side, together with Spain and Italy, while Germany, Switzerland, the Netherlands and the Scandinavian countries sit at the opposite end. As a result, a Brazilian and a German can leave the same meeting with different readings of what was agreed.

**Formal and informal.** We compared the same conversation about a new AI tool in two registers. Between friends, it goes like this: *"Did you see that new AI tool that dropped yesterday?"*. In a meeting, it goes like this: *"Have you had an opportunity to review the new artificial intelligence platform that was released yesterday?"*. The formal register avoids contractions and slang, uses more precise words and asks indirectly, with *"Could you please…?"* instead of *"Can you…?"*. Knowing how to switch registers is what separates a hallway chat from a client meeting.

**Networking.** A networking conversation has five steps: introduce yourself, ask about the other person's work, explain your own, find a common interest and wrap up with a next step, such as exchanging contacts or setting up a coffee. The last step is what turns a good conversation into a contact.

**Asking for and giving clarification.** Instead of pretending you understood, you ask: *"Just to be clear, are you saying that…?"* or *"Could you give an example?"*. And when you are the one who was unclear, there are ready-made phrases to rebuild the idea: *"What I mean is…"* or *"Let me put it another way."*

**Circumlocution.** This is talking around a word you forgot: describing what the thing does, what it looks like or what group it belongs to, instead of freezing or asking *"how do you say…?"*. We practiced with a game of Taboo using AI vocabulary, where you have to explain a term without saying it. For anyone who explains technology to people outside the field, this works in any language.

**Body language.** Point to the chart with an open palm instead of turning your back and pointing with a finger. Look at each person for two or three seconds before moving on to the next. Pause for two seconds after an important number. And replace "um" with a silent breath.

## And the English, of course

On the language side, the focus was on what trips up Brazilians most in technical presentations.

The first point was rhythm. English keeps time with stressed syllables: content words, such as nouns, main verbs and adjectives, carry the stress, while function words, such as articles, prepositions and pronouns, go by quickly. That is why *"Code drives the system"* and *"The code could have driven the system"* have the same three beats, and the small words squeeze in between them. For a Portuguese speaker, the challenge is precisely letting those words go by fast.

The second was the sounds that most give away an accent. The "th" has two versions, voiced (*this*) and voiceless (*think*). A final "s" can sound like [s], [z] or [iz], as in *looks*, *beds* and *messages*. And the past-tense "-ed" can become [t], [d] or [id], as in *cooked*, *cleaned* and *waited*. For practice, there were tongue twisters such as *"Those three thugs think that their brother is trustworthy."*

The third was organization. Transitions connect one idea to the next: *however*, *as a result*, *in addition*, *in conclusion*. In one exercise, we read the same text twice, first without transitions and then with them, and the second version was much easier to follow, even though the sentences were the same. Signposts play the same role across a whole presentation: *"My topic today is…"*, *"This talk is divided into…"*, *"Turning now to…"*, *"To sum up…"*. They tell the audience where you are and where you are going. Finally, we learned to describe data, starting with *"The bar chart compares…"* and using verbs that give the direction and speed of a change, such as *surge*, *plummet* and *level off*, or *sharply* and *gradually*.

## Three presentations, three formats

The course was built around three presentations, each with its own structure.

**1. Elevator pitch.** One to two minutes, or 75 to 150 words, in four parts. First, the introduction: a greeting, your name and, if possible, a hook. Then the explanation: what you do and what problem you solve. Next, the objective: what you are looking for and what value you offer. Finally, the call to action: the next step, such as setting up a call.

**2. Three-minute presentation.** We could choose between a research poster and a startup pitch. For the poster, the format was 30 seconds on the problem and context, 60 seconds on the method and the main result, pointing to the strongest chart, and 30 to 60 seconds on the conclusion, the impact and an invitation to ask questions. The pitch could be for an investor, with a hook, the customer's pain, the solution, what makes it different, proof and a next step, or for a partnership between companies, starting from a "shared enemy" that neither company can beat alone. This block left a lesson that this post follows, with the 30-second box at the top: prepare the same idea in three sizes, 30 seconds, three minutes and a long conversation, because you never know how much time the other person will give you.

**3. Academic presentation.** The third one was longer and more formal, about a piece of research. Mine comes next.

## My presentation: "What Makes a Second Brain Work? I Tested My Own"

On November 3, I gave an eight-minute talk in English about a study I ran on my own second brain method, the one I described in [AIOS and the Enterprise Second Brain](/blog/aios-enterprise-second-brain/) and use to give AI agents the context of a company. Instead of simply claiming that the method works, I made it compete with the alternatives: no knowledge base, all the documents pasted into the prompt, classic RAG, an Obsidian-style graph vault and an agent reading a plain folder with exactly the same text.

Almost everything the course taught went into it. In my script, every signpost was in bold, from *"This talk is divided into five parts"* to *"I'd now like to invite any questions."* The results slide opened with *"The bar chart compares…"*, just like in the class about data. In a preview session, the instructor reviewed only the form: structure, transitions and rhythm. In the final stretch, I cut the deck from 16 to 10 slides, about one per minute, because with 16 it felt like I was reading the slides. And for the Q&A, I prepared answers using the clarification phrases.

The result surprised me, and that is what I brought to the room. The agents that search and read the files on their own got almost everything right, well above the search pipelines. However, the extra organization of my method did not beat the same text in a plain folder. What did the work was the way the knowledge was written: every decision with its date and, when a rule changes, the change explained on the same page. That is why I closed the talk like this: *"I set out to prove my method. Instead, I found the part of it that seems to do the work: not how we file knowledge, but what we write down, and how."*
